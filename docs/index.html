R.O.A.D. Barbados Historic Handwriting

A from-scratch CRNN that reached 0.846 in ten days, and why the winners got to 0.93 with the two approaches the organisers' own starter pack recommended and we never ran.

The problem

Transcribe one handwritten line per image from 17th–19th century Barbados deeds and registers. There are 4,098 labelled training lines and 1,374 test lines, in ID,Target format. The score mixes word and character edit distance:

score = 1 − 0.5 × (W / 12 + C / 55)

W and C are the mean word and character edits per line. One wrong word costs about as much as 4.6 wrong characters, and getting one more word right on every line is worth about 0.04. Capitals, punctuation and the ^ superscript convention (Exec^rs, ad^rs) all count.

Three facts shaped the competition:

  • Old spellings are the target. prsents, coveniet, Conteyneing, theis: the transcript keeps what the clerk wrote. A model with a modern-English prior "corrects" these and loses points.
  • Two populations of image. About 75% of lines are clean register copies around 50 px tall. About 25% are large, stained or faded originals, up to 1,131 px tall and 6,051 px wide. Early models in the 26th-place write-up scored 0.892 on the first and 0.826 on the second.
  • The labels have a ceiling. Where two independent models agreed exactly (1,875 lines), the agreed text still scored only about 0.945 against the labels. The rest is annotator inconsistency: said/Said, a missing ^, a comma.

The organisers shipped a starter pack with three approaches: a vision-language model (Qwen2-VL-2B with LoRA, labelled "highest OCR accuracy"), Kraken ("historical and challenging documents") and PaddleOCR.

The final model

1. Split. One fixed fit/val split, grouped on exact transcript text so the 10 repeated legal formulas cannot leak. Validation on a fixed 120-line sample.
↓
2. Input. Grayscale, resized to 32 px high, width capped at 1,024 px, normalised to [−1, 1]. Training adds rotation (±3°), brightness, contrast, Gaussian noise and elastic distortion.
↓
3. Model. A CRNN trained from scratch: CNN with residual blocks, 2-layer BiLSTM, CTC loss. Dropout, AdamW, early stopping, ReduceLROnPlateau.
↓
4. Ensemble. Three seeds of the same recipe. Their per-frame probabilities are averaged before decoding.
↓
5. Decode. CTC prefix beam search (width 10), written and unit-tested from scratch, fused with a character 4-gram LM built from the training transcripts.

The discipline was fine. The problem was what it was pointed at.

The key finding: the model family was the competition

Both write-ups put most of the score on pretrained readers fine-tuned to read this handwriting, fused with CTC readers that keep the letters. The winner published the out-of-fold score of every reader on all 4,098 lines:

Our numbers are on 120 lines, theirs on 4,098 out-of-fold lines, so the comparison is approximate. The ranking is not in doubt. The 26th place reports the same pattern: Qwen3-VL-8B at 0.905, TrOCR-large at 0.878, a CTC reader at 0.816.

Three things stand out.

  • Where the LoRA goes matters more than whether you use it. The 26th place moved Qwen3-VL from 0.869 to 0.905 by adding LoRA to the vision tower. The winner wrote that "training the vision side mattered most". Our TrOCR LoRA adapted only the decoder's attention, the part that holds the modern-English prior we were fighting, and left the encoder that reads the ink frozen.
  • We generalised from one old model to a whole family. TrOCR-base (2021, RoBERTa decoder) failed, and the README concluded that "anything that has to learn or unlearn its own language model" would fail on 3,280 lines. The starter pack's Qwen2-VL was then dropped for "no zero-shot signal collected", a signal we never tried to collect.
  • Our own family had 0.07 of headroom. Kraken is a CTC reader with a character LM, the same design as our CRNN, and it scored 0.909 alone: within 0.01 of the VLMs. It trained on word-span crops and a taller input, with a 6-gram LM. Even the winner's plain ConvNeXt CTC at 128 px beat our 32 px CRNN by 0.02. The model family we chose was not the ceiling; the input size and the training data were.
  • Our approach was what the winners also needed. Kraken got the winner's largest vote (12 of 47), and a CTC reader at 0.816 alone was the 26th place's most valuable member (+0.0033 LB), because they keep old spellings that the VLMs modernise. What was missing was a strong, different reader to vote against ours.
  • Small was enough. LightOnOCR-2, a 1B model, scored 0.914 with a full fine-tune, level with the 8B and 9B Qwen readers. Every reader the winner used is Apache-2.0 (Kraken adds LGPL-2.1).

One held-out line shows the vote at work. On "the lett troble or molestacon", Qwen3-VL and LightOnOCR-2 (22 votes) read trouble. Qwen3.5, ConvNeXt and Kraken (23.8 votes; Kraken's 12 are spread over its 25 readings) read troble. The whole-line distance picked troble, 0.0296 against 0.0314, which matches the page. A VLM fine-tune is the strongest single reader and the one most likely to modernise. A CTC reader with a character LM keeps the letters. The vote needs both.

"No ideas left" was a list we had not finished reading

The project closed on Aug 10 with two of the organisers' three recommended approaches never run, one because of an environment conflict and one because of a guess about cost. That is not an exhausted idea list; it is an unread one. The same pattern followed in the next two projects: Kaggriculture stopped on Sep 3 with its close on Sep 30, and RSNA Knee stopped on Sep 1 with its close on Oct 22. Barbados was the first.

The compute question

The winner trained on 4 × RTX A6000 (48 GB), up to 21 hours per fold for the largest reader. We trained on a laptop RTX 4060 with 8 GB. Part of the gap is compute, and that should be said. But the winner also wrote that Qwen3-VL-8B matched the 32B model and that bigger models did not win. Their 1B reader (LightOnOCR-2) scored 0.914, Kraken scored 0.909 as a CTC model, and the starter pack's own choice was a 2B model. A 1–2B reader, a better CTC reader, or an 8B one in 4-bit on a rented 24–48 GB GPU, was within reach. We never priced it.

Handwriting OCR: what transfers

The technical lessons from this project and the two write-ups, for any line-level handwritten text recognition task with a small labelled set.

Lessons

Run the organisers' recommended approaches before ruling them out

The starter pack named three approaches and shipped training scripts for them. The winners used two of them in their modern form. We ran the third and a model that was not in the pack. A starter pack is the organisers' prior about what works on their data. Get a real number for each of its approaches, in its own environment, before dropping one.

One model's failure is not a family's verdict

TrOCR-base failed, and we concluded that pretrained text decoders fail on this data. The conclusion was about one 2021 model, at one input shape, adapted on one side. Before closing a family, try its current strongest member, and check that the adaptation touches the part of the model the failure points to.

A smoke test is not a result

A one-epoch run at 0.122 ended full fine-tuning. A smoke test answers "does it run?", not "is it worse?". Write down what a failure looks like before the run, and do not let a pipeline check decide an approach.

Ensemble across families, judged by contribution

More seeds of one recipe gave us +0.0095 and would have given little more. The winners' gains came from readers that make different mistakes, weighted by how much each one improves the combined result, so a weak model alone can carry the largest vote.

Validate on all the data you have

120 lines tracked the leaderboard, but could not separate ideas closer than about a hundredth. Both top finishers used out-of-fold predictions on all 4,098 lines and distrusted single folds. When the dataset is a few thousand rows, k-fold OOF costs k trainings and buys the only instrument fine enough to rank the last ideas.

One environment per conflicting toolkit

Kraken was lost to a dependency conflict in base. The starter pack already made one environment per approach; the winner kept vLLM in its own. When two toolkits pin different torch or numpy versions, give each its own environment and exchange files between them.

"No ideas left" means the list has not been read

The project closed with eight weeks left. Before closing, list every approach in the starter pack, in the forum and in the domain's standard toolbox, and mark each one run, priced, or ruled out with a number. If anything is unmarked, the project is not done.

What carried over well

Pinning the official metric from the forum on day one. Isolated, one-change experiments with logged negative results. A beam search checked against brute force before use. The decision not to normalise labels, and the rejection of the lexicon fix, both matched the winner's findings. The character LM in the CTC beam was the same idea as the winner's KenLM in Kraken.

Future: a rerun

If late submissions are accepted, each step can be checked against the hidden set; otherwise against five-fold OOF. Each step changes one thing and has a gate.

1. Folds. Five folds over all 4,098 lines, grouped by transcript and near-duplicate image. Re-score the current CRNN ensemble out-of-fold to get a baseline and a row-wise noise floor.
↓
2. A VLM reader. Qwen3-VL with LoRA in the vision tower and the LM (2B locally, 8B in 4-bit on a rented GPU), 2 epochs, original transcripts.
Gate: OOF above the CRNN's.
↓
3. Metric-aware vote. Pool n-best readings from the VLM and the CRNN and pick by weighted W/12 + C/55 distance.
Gate: OOF above the better single reader.
↓
4. Kraken + KenLM, in its own environment, as a third family.
Gate: the vote's OOF with and without it.
↓
5. Word positions. Forced alignment with the CTC reader, then word-span crops for the VLM.
Gate: OOF on the VLM alone, then on the vote.
